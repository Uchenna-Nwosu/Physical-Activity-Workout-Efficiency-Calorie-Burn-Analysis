# Physical-Activity-Workout-Efficiency-Calorie-Burn-Analysis

Project Overview

This project analyzes a fitness and health tracking dataset to understand how daily movement, active minutes, workout participation, stress, age, and workout type relate to calorie burn and activity efficiency.

The analysis was developed in Microsoft Power BI and presented through an interactive dashboard supported by pre-analysis planning, in-analysis interpretation, and post-analysis observations.

Business / Analytical Objective

The project was designed to answer questions such as:

How active are users on an average day?

Do more steps always translate into higher calorie burn?

How important are active minutes compared with step count?

Does workout type significantly change total calories burned?

Which activity level produces the best calorie efficiency?

How does workout frequency relate to stress?

Does age meaningfully reduce activity performance?

Is consistency in steps enough to guarantee higher calorie burn?

Key Findings

1. Activity Was Consistent, but Calorie Burn Varied

Users averaged approximately 11,000 daily steps, 71 active minutes, and a 75% workout participation rate.

Despite relatively consistent activity, calorie outcomes remained clustered around approximately 2,750 calories, suggesting that more movement did not always translate into proportional increases in calorie burn.

2. Step Count Alone Was Not a Complete Performance Indicator

The daily steps and active-minutes analysis showed that high step counts did not always coincide with high active minutes.

This indicates an important difference between:

low-intensity movement accumulated throughout the day; and

concentrated, higher-intensity activity.

The dashboard therefore suggests that active minutes add important context to step count.

3. Workout Participation Was Stable

Daily workout participation remained broadly between approximately 73% and 77%.

The absence of major drops suggests strong and consistent engagement across the reporting period.

4. Workout Type Had Limited Impact on Total Daily Calories

Average calorie burn was very similar across workout categories:

None — approximately 2.753K

Yoga — approximately 2.751K

Strength — approximately 2.745K

Cardio — approximately 2.742K

This suggests that total daily calorie burn was influenced by overall activity composition rather than workout label alone.

5. Calorie Burn Was Balanced Across Workout Types

Within the displayed calorie-burn ranges, Cardio, Strength, Yoga, and None each contributed fairly similar proportions.

No single workout type dominated the higher calorie ranges.

6. Medium Activity Produced the Best Efficiency

The efficiency analysis indicates that medium activity levels produced the highest calories burned per 1,000 steps, while high activity showed diminishing efficiency.

This suggests that maximum activity volume is not necessarily the most efficient approach.

7. Stress Was Stable Across Workout Frequency

Average stress levels were very similar across activity-frequency bands:

High — approximately 5.49

Moderate — approximately 5.54

Low — approximately 5.65

The overall average stress level was 5.50.

The dashboard suggests that more frequent workouts did not automatically result in lower stress and that recovery and individual tolerance may be important.

8. Age Did Not Eliminate Performance

Average active minutes were highly consistent across age groups.

Average calorie burn was also broadly similar, with older users achieving outcomes comparable to, and in some cases slightly above, younger groups.

This suggests that age changed how activity was achieved more than whether meaningful performance was possible.

9. Consistency Was Necessary but Not Sufficient

The scatter plot comparing average daily steps with average calories burned showed a wide spread in calories at similar step counts.

This suggests that step consistency alone does not fully explain performance.

Intensity, active minutes, workout mix, and individual differences also matter.

Recommendations

Use more than step count to evaluate fitness performance

Combine steps with active minutes, workout intensity, and calorie efficiency.

Prioritize sustainable, moderate activity

Medium activity levels showed stronger efficiency than very high activity levels.

Avoid over-relying on workout labels

Total activity composition appears more important for overall calorie burn than whether a session is labelled Cardio, Strength, or Yoga.

Monitor stress and recovery for high-frequency exercisers

Stable average stress does not eliminate individual variation or recovery risk.

Personalize activity goals by age and ability

Older age groups demonstrated comparable outcomes, suggesting that pacing and efficiency may be more useful than assuming reduced capability.

Track consistency alongside intensity

Regular movement is important, but performance improves when consistency is paired with sufficient activity quality.

Conclusion

The analysis shows that the population is consistently active, but more activity does not automatically mean greater efficiency or proportionally higher calorie burn.

The strongest overall message from the dashboard is that fitness performance is multi-factorial. Step count, active minutes, intensity, workout composition, recovery, age, and consistency all contribute to the outcome.

The Power BI dashboard provides an interactive view of these relationships and helps move the analysis beyond simple activity totals toward a more balanced understanding of workout efficiency and physical performance.
